The scenario

Meet Fernbird Coffee Co.

A small specialty roaster. They sell online (one-off orders and subscriptions), supply local cafés, and run a stall at the weekend market. Business is growing, and the way they keep track of it hasn't kept up.

Numbers live in five places

Online sales in the store, subscriptions in another tool, café orders in a shared spreadsheet. Every Monday someone stitches them together by hand.

Stock runs low unnoticed

The first sign a bag is running out is usually that it sells out, and a roast takes a couple of weeks to replace.

Cash and customers go quiet

Overdue invoices and cafés that have stopped reordering only surface when someone happens to remember.

Automations

Then take the repetitive part off someone's plate.

A dashboard is something you have to remember to open. These run on their own and tell you when something needs attention. Here are four a business like Fernbird might use.

Monday briefing

  1. Trigger Every Monday, 7:00am
  2. Reads Sales, orders, stock, invoices
  3. Sends A one-page email to the owner

Replaces the Monday spreadsheet shuffle. (Example: about 2 hours a week.)

Reorder alert

  1. Trigger Stock cover drops below lead time + a buffer
  2. Reads Stock levels and recent sales rate
  3. Sends A message with a suggested roast quantity

Catches stock-outs before customers do.

Overdue nudge

  1. Trigger An invoice is 7+ days late
  2. Reads The customer, amount and order history
  3. Drafts A polite reminder for a person to approve

A human stays in the loop: it drafts, you send.

Export tidy-up

  1. Trigger A new CSV lands in a shared folder
  2. Does Standardises columns, removes duplicates, flags odd rows
  3. Writes One clean master sheet that dashboards read from

The unglamorous step that makes everything else trustworthy.
